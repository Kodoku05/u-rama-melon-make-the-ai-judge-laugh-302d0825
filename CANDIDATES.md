# Office joke workshop

Scores are editorial judgments, not model evaluations. Each criterion is scored
1–5: office joke (J), true observation (T), surprising turn (S), tightness (C),
originality (O). The criteria are considered in that order, not simply summed.
Originality scores express confidence in freshness, not a claim of exhaustive search.

## Candidates

1. **Our CEO asked everyone to submit an example of pointless bureaucracy. HR rejected mine because I used the wrong form.**
   J 5 / T 5 / S 5 / C 4 / O 4.
   Gates: concrete CEO request and HR rejection; a valid complaint becomes invalid
   paperwork. The final word, “form,” supplies a flat administrative verdict.
   No pun, stock opening, judge address, or explanation. Fits the target length.

2. **The CEO asked what we wanted in the new office. We said raises. He put 'Raises' on a meeting room.**
   J 5 / T 4 / S 4 / C 5 / O 4.
   Gates: concrete CEO scene; symbolic recognition substitutes for money; deadpan
   room designation. No pun or stock opening. Fits the target length, but the
   literal substitution feels more engineered than candidate 1.

3. **Our CEO asked what kept us awake at night. HR added the answers to our job descriptions.**
   J 4 / T 4 / S 5 / C 5 / O 4.
   Gates: CEO and HR create a recognizable escalation of obligations; closes on
   an administrative document. No pun or stock setup. Slightly below the length
   target; the source of stress is less concrete than candidate 1.

4. **Our boss made every meeting optional. HR now calls attendance 'a useful indicator of commitment.'**
   J 4 / T 5 / S 3 / C 4 / O 3.
   Gates: boss and HR, recognizable coercion, bureaucratic closing verdict.
   No pun; target length. The optional-but-required observation is familiar,
   so the turn risks being anticipated.

5. **Our CEO asked for brutal honesty. HR scheduled it for his vacation.**
   J 4 / T 4 / S 4 / C 5 / O 4.
   Gates: CEO scene; HR technically schedules honesty while avoiding its audience;
   deadpan final word. No pun or stock opening. Below target length and gives
   less room for recognition before the turn.

6. **My boss asked what would help us leave on time. I said fewer meetings. He booked an hour to discuss my time management.**
   J 5 / T 5 / S 2 / C 3 / O 2.
   Gates: concrete boss conversation, perverse corrective action, bureaucratic
   ending. Target length and no pun. Reject: meeting-about-meetings is a stock
   mechanism and the extra sentence makes the punchline easy to predict.

7. **The CEO told us to stop asking permission. HR sent a form to opt in.**
   J 4 / T 5 / S 4 / C 5 / O 3.
   Gates: CEO instruction is defeated by HR's procedure; “opt in” is a flat
   administrative beat. No pun or judge address. Below target length; the
   permission-to-stop-asking-permission contradiction is familiar.

## Selection and one tightness revision

Candidate 1 wins: a complaint about bureaucracy is itself rejected by bureaucracy.
The rejection is technically defensible and defeats the CEO's stated goal.

Draft: Our CEO asked everyone to submit an example of pointless bureaucracy. HR rejected mine because I used the wrong form.

Final: The CEO asked for examples of pointless bureaucracy. HR rejected mine for using the wrong form.

Cut “everyone to submit” because the request already implies submission; use
“examples” to shorten “an example”; cut “because I” in the rejection. “The CEO”
sets the scene without an unnecessary possessive. Keep “pointless” because it
establishes why the form requirement is the joke. Keep the final word “form”:
the sentence ends on the concrete bureaucratic obstacle, with no explanation.
